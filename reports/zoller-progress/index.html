01 — The brief

Weeks of typing
before a single
item went live.

Every auction meant 1,250 lots entered by hand. Photograph, edit, renumber, then type a title, a description, a start price and an estimate range — one lot at a time, to lot 1,250.

The work was not hard. It was long, and it happened before anything could be sold, on every single auction.

The job we took: remove the typing, not the judgement. The platform drafts every row; a human still signs off on anything the model is not sure about.

04 — What the data showed

Estimates
were high.

Realized prices sat consistently below estimates across the historical set — systematic estimate inflation, invisible until 32,000 records were in one place.

This was not in the brief. It surfaced because the corpus exists, and it is worth more than the time saving: an estimate range that tracks reality changes what bidders do before the hammer.

07 — The vision

Cataloguing was
the first job.

The corpus is the asset. Once 32,000 items with their realized prices sit in one place, the listing engine is only the first thing you can build on top of it.

Acquisition, priced

Inventory Watch already sees 21 dealer sites. The next step is scoring what it finds against realized prices — not "this is for sale", but "this is for sale below what it makes".

Built on: corpus + watch
Estimates as a discipline

Every auction feeds its results back in. Estimates stop being a judgement call repeated from last time and start being a number that corrects itself.

Built on: corpus
Catalogue on the day

When cataloguing costs hours instead of weeks, the constraint on how often you run an auction stops being data entry. That is the real unlock, and it is a business decision, not a build one.

Built on: the engine
One platform, not three tools

Listing, review and cross-posting already live in one hosted PWA. Inventory and estimate intelligence join it rather than becoming another login.
